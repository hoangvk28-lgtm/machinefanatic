# Research: carpet cleaners, steam cleaners, steam mops, garment steamers

Buyer-knowledge notes for guide writers. Based on 8 web searches (retail and brand buying guides, Consumer Reports summaries, Vacuum Wars, cleaning-industry blogs). Many sources are affiliate or cleaning-company sites, so numbers are rules of thumb; say "check the manual" where flagged. No prices. Use as category-level facts; always tie to the real named machines from the compact.json.

## Upright carpet cleaners (deep cleaners)
- Tank layout: most have two tanks, one for clean water plus solution and one for dirty water. Consumer Reports testers found two separate tanks more convenient than a single combined tank.
- Tank size: a clean tank of 1 gallon or more means fewer trips to the sink but adds weight. Larger tank = more floor covered per fill.
- Weight example from retail listings: a 1 gallon Bissell TurboClean at 16.3 lb versus a larger TurboClean Pet XL at 23.3 lb. Weight matters for stairs and carrying.
- Brushes: brush rolls drive cleaning power. Multiple rows of bristles reach embedded dirt; Consumer Reports says an independently rotating brush scrubs more aggressively than a fixed brush. A wider cleaning path saves time in big rooms.
- Heat: heated cleaning is rare and sold as a premium feature. Pro-style machines heat water to roughly 150 to 200 F, which helps break down oily residue and speeds drying. Heated drying is the fastest way to dry, but only a few models offer it.
- Dry time: never promise "instantly dry". Drying depends on pile, ventilation, amount of solution used and extraction strength. A dry-only (suction only) pass or setting pulls out more water after the wash and can shorten drying. Consumer Reports measures leftover moisture because damp carpet leads to mold or mildew.
- Convenience score factors (Consumer Reports): weight, cord length, coverage area, tank access and ease of emptying. Top scorers are lighter with longer cords and simple tank access.
- Upright vs spot: uprights suit whole rooms; spot cleaners suit stairs, upholstery, cars and fresh spills.
- Low-moisture methods (encapsulation, dry compound) exist for delicate or moisture-sensitive fibers where a wet extractor is a poor fit.

## Portable spot and upholstery cleaners
- Process: spray solution, agitate with a brush, then vacuum the dirty water into a small recovery tank.
- Hose length (Vacuum Wars lab data): ranged from 6.5 ft (Kenmore Spotlite Go, best) and 6.4 ft (Vacmaster 400 Series) down to under 4 ft (Dreame N10). A hose under 4 ft makes stairs and car seats awkward.
- Consumer Reports tip for stairs: check that cord and hose length let you clean without balancing the machine on a narrow step.
- Hose kinking is a tested quality point (Zelpio scored it); a stiff, short or kinking hose is a real annoyance.
- Cordless spot cleaners trade run time for freedom of movement; check tank capacity, hose length and cleaning width against the job.
- Common accessories: extendable hose, upholstery tool, scrub brush. Check which tools ship in the box before comparing.
- Smaller, lighter models are easier for people who worry about weight.

## Steam cleaners (canister and handheld)
- Pressure: sources quote different minimums. Common thresholds are 3.5 bar (about 50 psi) at the high end, 40 plus psi as a baseline, and 50 plus psi to push steam into tile pores and fabric fibers rather than just wetting the surface. Higher pressure with a hotter boiler cleans faster.
- Temperature: one guide treats 200 F as a minimum and 240 F plus as commercial-grade. Many listings omit temperature, so say so when the spec is missing.
- Canister models: largest tanks and highest pressure, suited to whole-home work across several surfaces.
- Handheld models: lighter and heat faster, but a tank typically lasts only about 10 to 20 minutes before a refill.
- Water: tap water is usually fine unless the water is hard; in hard-water areas use distilled water to protect the boiler from mineral scale. Descale with the maker's solution or a water and vinegar mix if the manual allows it. Built-in descaling cartridges reduce long-term maintenance.
- Safety: steam burns skin, keep the nozzle pointed away, wear eye protection, keep tools away from outlets.
- Do not steam: unsealed grout (heat and moisture can erode or crumble it; sealed grout is generally fine), unsealed hardwood, silk, velvet, velour, damask, suede and faux leather (crinkling or discoloration reported).
- Colored rugs: one pro cleaner reported yellow dye bleeding onto the white areas of a multicolor rug. Spot-test on a hidden area, use the lowest heat and steam settings and keep the nozzle moving. Sources had nothing specific on wool rugs, so tell readers to follow the care label or ask a rug cleaner.
- Upholstery: test an inconspicuous spot (back of the couch, underside of a drape) first and avoid soaking the fabric.

## Steam mops
- Floor safety: Consumer Reports warns against steaming unsealed hardwood, engineered wood or other heat and moisture sensitive floors because of warping over time. Quick check: if water beads on the surface the finish is sealed, if it soaks in the floor is at risk. The maker's floor-type wording on the box is the best signal; several models are rated for sealed laminate and hardwood.
- Temperature range across tested mops: about 131 F on a low-end unit to roughly 284 F on a large corded model. A multi-setting or low-steam mode is the safer choice for wood and laminate; high steam on laminate can cause streaking.
- Heat-up: fast models are ready in about 15 to 30 seconds; an older heavy style took about 8 minutes.
- Tank: about 15 oz is enough for a typical 800 sq ft floor according to one guide. Ease of filling (removable tank) mattered more to reviewers than raw capacity; non-removable tanks are a common complaint.
- Water: manufacturers often recommend distilled water to avoid mineral build-up (Consumer Reports).
- Practical: sweep or vacuum first because steam mops do not pick up debris; microfiber pads get dirty fast so extra pads are worth having.
- Myth: "steam cleans every floor". It does not; unsealed wood, some vinyl planks and waxed floors are at risk.

## Garment steamers
- Steamer vs iron: a steamer relies on steam alone from a wand or head and works on hanging clothes; an iron combines steam with heat and pressure. Steamers are gentle on a wide range of fabrics; irons win for crisp creases, collars, pleats and shirt cuffs.
- Wattage: sources cite 1,000 to 2,000 W overall; 1,200 to 1,800 W gives faster heat-up and denser steam. For handhelds look for about 1,200 W or more.
- Heat-up: about 30 seconds is the sweet spot for handhelds; larger standing models with big tanks often take 45 to 60 seconds but run much longer.
- Tank: handheld tanks are typically 215 to 300 ml; a 260 ml tank gave about 10 to 15 minutes of steaming in one example. A detachable tank is easier to fill and clean. Bigger is not automatically better in practice because it adds weight.
- Upright (standing) vs handheld: standing models give longer run time and a hanger or pole; handhelds are lighter, better for travel and quick touch-ups.
- Fabrics: polyester and nylon need lower heat than cotton and linen when ironing; some steam irons have a dry-iron mode for silk and heat-sensitive fabrics. Avoid blasting velvet, suede and silk with direct steam.

## Mold, odor and common mistakes
- Overwetting soaks the carpet pad and subfloor and encourages mold and mildew. A post-cleaning mold smell typically means carpet and pad did not dry within about 24 hours (restoration company claim, treat as rule of thumb).
- Leftover soap residue attracts dirt and leads to faster re-soiling; rinse with a clear-water pass.
- Laundry detergent is a poor carpet solution because it cannot be rinsed out fully; use a formula made for the machine.
- The IICRC lists using the wrong cleaning product as a cause of permanent damage and discoloration.
- Run fans and open windows after cleaning; make multiple dry passes to cut moisture.
- Warranty and formula voiding: the searches found no source confirming this, so writers should only say "use the formula and descaling method the manual lists" without claiming a warranty is voided.
- Common buyer mistakes: buying for tank size without checking weight; ignoring hose length for stairs; assuming a steam mop is safe on all floors; skipping distilled water in hard-water homes; skipping a hidden-spot colorfastness test.
