# Research: outdoor griddles and flat tops (popmech gap round 17)
Collected 2026-11-12 by web search. Numbers below come from the sources listed at the end; do not invent others.
For small indoor electric griddles see docs/research/electric-griddles.md.

## Plate steel is the spec that decides everything
- Cold-rolled carbon steel in the **4 mm to 6 mm** range spreads heat edge to edge and holds a seasoning layer; thinner plates (sources quote 3 mm to 5 mm as typical) warp and spike.
- Thickness is what stops a plate deforming when frozen patties hit a screaming-hot surface. A 28 inch Weber Slate is quoted at about 7 mm, at the heavy end of consumer plates.
- Plate thickness and burner count correlate with cooking quality far better than total BTU does.
- Stainless and coated plates need less oil and resist rust and scratches better than bare cold-rolled steel, at the cost of the classic griddle patina.

## Seasoning and rust: the real ownership cost
- Bare cold-rolled steel is **not non-stick until it is seasoned**, and it rusts quickly if left wet or unoiled after a cook.
- Routine: scrape hot, wipe, thin oil coat, cover. A hard lid or hood helps but a cover is still needed outdoors.
- Rust is the single most common reason an outdoor griddle gets replaced, not burner failure.

## Burners, zones and BTU
- Chasing maximum BTU leads buyers to thin plates and uneven heat. **Independent heat zones matter more**: they let you sear on one end and hold at a simmer on the other.
- A griddle with 3 independently controlled zones over roughly 594 sq in (a 33 inch class plate) is a realistic reference point for zone density.
- Count how many burners you can actually run separately, not how many burner knobs exist.

## Size: what each width really gives you
- Advertised width is not cooking area. Compare measured square inches: a 36 inch class plate is around **720 sq in**; a 28 inch class plate lands around **500 to 520 sq in**; a 22 inch tabletop is far smaller again.
- 36 inch: cooks for a crowd, usually 4 burners, needs a cart and a parking spot. 28 inch: the common 2 to 3 burner family size for a normal patio. 22 inch and under: tabletop, 1 to 2 burners, tailgating and camping.
- A hood or hard lid adds steaming and melting ability and protects the plate between cooks; it also raises the stored height.

## Buying mistakes
- Buying on total BTU or on advertised inches instead of measured square inches plus plate thickness.
- Assuming a griddle replaces a grill. There are no grate marks and no fat drip-off; a grill-and-griddle combo exists precisely because the two surfaces do different jobs.
- Ignoring grease management: a rear grease chute with a large catch cup is far easier to live with than a small centre hole.
- Forgetting that a bare-steel plate left through a wet winter without a cover will pit.
