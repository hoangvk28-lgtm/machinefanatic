# Research: grills (popmech gap round 17)
Collected 2026-11-12 by web search. Numbers below come from the sources listed at the end; do not invent others.

## BTU: the number buyers read wrong
- Total BTU on its own means almost nothing. The useful figure is **heat flux: primary-burner BTU divided by primary cooking area**.
- Practical sweet spot: roughly **80 to 100 BTU per square inch**. Worked example from the sources: 60,000 BTU over 600 sq in (100 BTU/sq in) out-cooks 80,000 BTU over 1,000 sq in (80 BTU/sq in).
- Well above 100 BTU/sq in is usually marketing, and a higher burner rating simply burns more propane for the same steak.
- Build quality (lid and body mass, heat retention, burner metal) matters more than any BTU figure; a grill that traps heat beats a grill that advertises it.

## Sizing and burner count
- Allow roughly **70 to 80 sq in of primary grate per person eating**.
- Family of 3 to 5: a three-burner cabinet with **450 sq in or more primary area and about 30,000 to 32,000 BTU** is the usual landing spot.
- Burner count is secondary to area and to having enough burners to make a two-zone fire. A 2-burner can do two-zone; a 1-burner cannot. Five and six burner units are mostly about width and side burners, not better cooking.
- Watch how brands pad the "total cooking area" figure with a warming rack you cannot sear on. Compare **primary** area only.

## Pellet grills
- **PID controller is the feature to pay for.** PID feeds pellets continuously and typically holds within about 5 to 10 F of the set point; cheap on/off controllers swing roughly 20 to 50 F depending on the source.
- **Hopper capacity sets unattended cook length:** about 20 lb lasts roughly 12 to 18 hours at 225 F; a 30 lb hopper can pass 24 hours.
- Pellet units need mains power for controller, auger and fan, so they are tethered to an outlet or an inverter.
- Known failure points: **auger jams from moisture-swollen pellets, igniter burnout, fan motor failure** - all worse in humid climates. Store pellets sealed and dry.
- Heavy smoke flavour comes from low-temperature cooks. A pellet grill run hot and fast tastes much lighter than buyers expect.
- Check whether the hopper can be emptied easily (a clean-out chute) if you switch wood types or store the grill wet.

## Charcoal and kamado
- Charcoal kettles and kamados win on searing heat and on flavour; they lose on convenience.
- A kamado's ceramic mass holds temperature for very long cooks with little fuel, but vents and charcoal are managed by hand. It is a low-and-slow machine that can also sear, not a weeknight burger machine.
- For brisket and ribs, kamado and pellet are both sensible. For burgers and steaks, charcoal kettle or gas is the better tool.
- Cheap thin-walled charcoal grills swing with the wind; lid fit and vent quality decide whether you can hold 250 F at all.

## Electric and apartment or balcony use
- Electric is the type most often permitted on a balcony because there is no open flame, but permission depends on **three separate things: local fire code, the lease or HOA rules, and the unit's own UL listing**. Any one of them can say no.
- Propane is the most restricted. In New York it is illegal to store a standard backyard propane barbecue on a balcony, roof deck, roof, rear yard or courtyard, and some states bar even storing a grill on a balcony.
- Tabletop and camping grills are not a loophole: building rules apply to them by fuel type, not by size.
- No outdoor grill, electric included, is safe indoors: carbon monoxide and fire risk. Only appliances rated for indoor use belong inside.
- Electric grills trade peak heat for convenience; expect good results on vegetables, chicken and sausages and weaker crust on a thick steak than gas or charcoal.
