# Research: pizza ovens (popmech gap round 16)
Collected 2026-11-12 by web search. Numbers below come from the sources listed at the end; do not invent others.

## Fuel types
- **Gas (propane):** heats in roughly **15 to 25 minutes**, holds a steady deck temperature, no fire tending. The convenience choice and the easiest to run on a weeknight.
- **Wood-fired:** needs **45 minutes or more** (some sources say up to 2 hours) to reach cooking temperature, and the fire must be fed between bakes. Credited with flavour, though over a roughly 90-second Neapolitan bake the smoky difference is small - buy wood for the ritual and the live flame, not because the pizza is dramatically different.
- **Multi-fuel / dual-fuel:** a wood-and-charcoal tray plus a bolt-on gas burner. Worth it only if you will actually use both; the burner is often an extra purchase.
- **Pellet:** a hopper feeds a small firebox. Easier than splits, but the hopper needs refilling every 15 to 20 minutes of cooking and ash management is still manual.
- **Electric:** the only type rated for indoor use, because there is no open flame and no fuel storage, and no combustion fumes. Peak temperature is lower than gas or wood but recovery is predictable.

## Temperature: what actually matters
- **700 F+ deck temperature** is the practical benchmark for Neapolitan-style crust. Higher peaks exist (the Ooni Karu 12 is rated to 950 F) but 900 F is not a universal spec and should not be treated as a requirement.
- Peak temperature alone is a bad buying criterion. What matters is whether the oven can **sustain** enough stored heat to set the crust before the toppings dry out. Thermal mass and recovery beat a high number on the box.
- Indoor electric units commonly land at 700 to 850 F, which is enough for a 3 to 5 minute pie, not a 60-second one.

## Stone and thermal mass
- Stone thickness: **1 inch minimum, 1.5 inches preferred**. A 15 mm cordierite stone holds heat between pies noticeably better than a 10 mm one.
- **Recovery between pizzas is the number most buyers ignore:** allow **3 to 4 minutes** between bakes on a stone-based oven, longer on a thin stone. Cooking for a crowd is a recovery problem, not a capacity problem.
- Cooldown is slow too; a hot stone stays untouchable long after the burner is off, which matters for storage and covers.

## Size: 12 inch vs 16 inch
- The 12 inch models take a 12 inch pie; the 16 inch models take 16 inches and cook more forgivingly because there is room to turn the pizza.
- Weight roughly doubles: the Ooni Koda 16 is about 40 lb against about 20 lb for the Koda 12. A 12 inch oven is a genuinely portable, one-hand carry; a 16 inch oven is a patio fixture.
- Larger chambers need a higher BTU burner to hit the same peak temperature, so do not compare a big cheap oven with a small expensive one on temperature alone.

## Common buyer mistakes
- Buying on peak temperature instead of sustained heat and recovery.
- Underestimating footprint: a countertop indoor oven can swallow half a worktop plus a whole cabinet shelf when stored.
- Assuming an outdoor oven can move indoors. Only electric models are rated for indoor use; gas and wood need open air for combustion fumes. Even weatherproof electric hybrids need a roof - none of them can be run in rain or snow.
- Forgetting the peel and the turning peel. A 16 inch bake needs a turning peel, not just a launch peel.
- No interior light on compact models makes judging browning hard, especially for a dark wood-fired chamber.
- Capacity: indoor units nearly always cook one pizza at a time; only the large outdoor ovens take more than one.
