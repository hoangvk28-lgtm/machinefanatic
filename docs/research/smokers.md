# Research: smokers (popmech gap round 16)
Collected 2026-11-12 by web search. Numbers below come from the sources listed at the end; do not invent others.

## Types and the real tradeoff
- **Electric:** easiest to run, usable in more locations, easy to clean, but depends on mains power and the heating element/controller is the usual failure point. Weakest smoke flavour of the four: the element produces heat, the wood chip tray produces only a modest amount of smoke. Lowest learning curve.
- **Propane (gas vertical):** steady heat without wood management, more temperature flexibility than electric, generally smaller and more portable. Needs tank swaps and the flavour is close to neutral. Some cooks report a propane smell carrying into food on cheap burners.
- **Pellet:** the middle ground. Auger plus controller gives set-and-forget running for long unattended cooks (overnight brisket), with real wood flavour. Most pellet units have a low "smoke mode" that drops the temperature to produce heavier mild smoke. Still needs an outlet, and pellets must stay dry.
- **Offset (charcoal/stick burner):** best smoke flavour, most hands-on. Requires continuous fire management; thin-walled offsets need constant attention because they cannot buffer heat. Charcoal offsets are the cheapest way into real stick-burner flavour.
- **Charcoal vertical (bullet/drum):** cheap, simple, good flavour, but temperature is controlled only by vents and fuel load.

## Sizing: the biggest buying trap
- "More square inches is better" is the most common mistake; brands advertise total surface across all racks, including racks you cannot load with a thick cut.
- Practical target: roughly **400 to 500 sq in total cooking area for 2 to 4 people** - handles a full rack of ribs or a 10 lb pork butt.
- Rack spacing matters more than total area for a pork butt or turkey; count usable racks, not advertised area.

## Steel and build
- Thin metal cannot hold heat: single-wall construction struggles in cold weather and swings badly in wind.
- Heavy-gauge steel or porcelain-coated bodies buffer temperature swings; this is the single biggest difference between a $200 and a $600 vertical.
- Door and lid seal quality determines how much smoke and heat escape; sprung latches and a flush door beat a loose hinged lid.

## Temperature range: test both ends
- Ask whether the unit can reach **350 to 500 F** for poultry (crisp skin on turkey) and whether it can hold **near 100 F** for cold smoking cheese or fish. Many electric units top out around 275 F, which is fine for ribs and useless for turkey skin.
- Controller type matters: a dial thermostat drifts, a PID controller holds a set point far tighter.

## Features worth paying for
- **Built-in meat probe / probe port** on the side so a remote thermometer's wires are not pinched by the door.
- **Automatic shut-off and cool-down** that triggers when the meat hits its target internal temperature (electric units).
- **Side chip loader** on electric smokers: lets you add wood without opening the main door and dumping the heat.
- **WiFi/app control** is genuinely useful on pellet units for overnight cooks, where the point is not opening the lid at 3 am.
- Lid-mounted dome thermometers read air temperature at the dome, not at grate level; treat them as a rough guide only.
