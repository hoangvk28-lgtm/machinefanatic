# Vacuums research (cordless stick, handheld, upright, canister, commercial)
Written 2026-11-14 from web searches (Vacuum Wars and TechRadar cordless buyer guides, Consumer Reports / Tom's Guide / TechRadar / Sylvane upright vs canister guides, CleanLink coverage of the CRI Seal of Approval vacuum program). Reuse for every round-29 vacuum guide; also see mini-vacuums.md (handheld) and wet-dry-vacuums.md.

## Suction numbers
- There is no universal standard for suction. Pa (pascals) is pressure at the inlet; air watts combine pressure and airflow, which is what actually carries dirt. Brands measure differently and often at the motor, not at the cleaning head, so a "60KPA" listing from one seller cannot be compared with another brand's figure.
- Budget no-name sticks listing 40,000 to 60,000 Pa and 650W are the norm on Amazon; treat those as marketing ceilings. A low standard-mode air watt figure (around 30 AW) is too weak for carpet.
- For carpet, airflow plus a motorized brushroll matters more than the peak number.

## Runtime and battery
- Headline runtime is almost always the eco/lowest mode, often with a non-motorized tool. Boost/turbo commonly drains a pack in 10 to 15 minutes. Check the standard-mode figure; 25 to 30 minutes in normal mode is a reasonable benchmark for a whole-home stick.
- Lithium-ion packs degrade in roughly 2 to 5 years. A click-in removable battery that the maker actually sells as a spare keeps the vacuum alive; a sealed pack can end its life. Two-battery bundles double real runtime.
- Tool-platform vacuums (DeWalt 20V, Milwaukee M12/M18, Black+Decker, Ryobi) are often sold bare tool: battery and charger extra unless the title says kit.

## Filtration and allergies
- HEPA filter alone is not enough: a sealed system (housing tested for leaks at seams) keeps fine dust from bypassing the filter. A sealed HEPA vacuum with a bag is the usual recommendation for serious allergies or asthma.
- Emptying a bagless bin kicks dust back into the air; bagged units contain it. Consumer Reports found bagged models tend to deep-clean better and are less messy to empty. Bagless saves on recurring bag cost.

## Upright vs canister
- Uprights: wide path, strong on large carpeted areas, easy to store standing, but heavier and bulkier on stairs and under furniture.
- Canisters: lighter wand in the hand, motor in a separate shell that muffles noise, better on stairs, drapes, under beds. A canister for carpet needs a powerhead (motorized/electric brush); air-driven turbo heads are weaker; pure-suction (no beater bar) heads suit hardwood, rugs with fringe and delicate wool.
- Lift-away uprights blur the line: pod detaches for stairs.

## Commercial vacuums
- CRI Seal of Approval / Green Label vacuum program (Carpet and Rug Institute) tests three things: soil removal (Bronze 40 to 49 percent, Silver 50 to 54, Gold 55+, per program levels reported 2008 to 2010), dust containment (Gold stricter on airborne dust released), and carpet texture retention (no more than one step of change after a year of use). It does not test durability. Thresholds may have changed; state them as program levels, not current guarantees.
- Commercial uprights (Sanitaire, Oreck Commercial, ProTeam, Bissell BigGreen): long cords (often 40 ft or more), metal or reinforced parts, bagged, simple single-motor designs that are cheap to repair, belts and brushrolls replaceable.

## Cost of ownership
- A long-lived machine at a higher price can cost less per year than a cheap one replaced every 2 to 3 years. Budget recurring parts: bags, filters, belts, batteries.

## Common buyer mistakes
- Buying on peak Pa or eco runtime.
- Ignoring whether spare batteries exist.
- Assuming "HEPA filter" means sealed.
- Buying a heavy upright for a home with stairs, or a suction-only canister for wall-to-wall carpet.
- Bare-tool listings mistaken for complete kits.
