A label argument that costs shoppers money

Search for an air purifier and you meet a wall of near-identical claims. One says HEPA. Another says True HEPA. A third says HEPA-type, a fourth H13, a fifth medical grade. It is natural to assume they form a ladder, with True HEPA a rung above plain HEPA.

They do not form a ladder, and the confusion is partly deliberate. The phrases are mostly branding layered on top of one real engineering definition. Once you separate the definition from the branding, you can read any product page in about thirty seconds.

What HEPA actually describes

HEPA stands for high efficiency particulate air. It describes a class of filter media defined by how well it captures particles of a specific difficult size. The commonly cited requirement for the US definition is that the filter capture at least 99.97 percent of particles at 0.3 micron. Other regions use related classification systems, such as European classes with their own test methods and numbers.

The reference size is not an accident. Particles around that size tend to be the hardest for a fiber filter to capture, because they fall between two capture mechanisms. Larger particles are caught by impact and interception, and smaller ones are caught by diffusion, as they wander randomly until they hit a fiber. Testing at the hardest size gives a conservative result.

The test describes the filter medium under a stated airflow. It does not by itself prove that a whole purifier removes that fraction of particles from a room, because room air also has to be moved through the filter and not around it.

Where the phrase True HEPA comes from

True HEPA has no separate legal definition. It arose in marketing to separate genuine HEPA filters from cheaper products that borrowed the name. Some sellers used phrases such as HEPA-type, HEPA-style or HEPA-like for filters that do not meet the efficiency definition, and the word true was a way to say that this one is the real thing.

That makes the phrase useful as a clue and useless as proof. A product described as True HEPA is claiming to meet the standard. A product described as HEPA-type is carefully avoiding that claim. The trouble is that neither phrase is a certificate, so you still need to look at what the manufacturer states about testing.

Reading tip: a qualifier before or after the word HEPA, such as type, style, like, grade or equivalent, is a signal that the filter is not claimed to meet the HEPA definition.

European classes and why higher is not always needed

European standards grade filters by classes such as H13 and H14, with an efficiency measured at the most penetrating particle size for the filter and a separate figure for local leakage. Higher classes capture a larger share of the hardest particles.

For a home, the difference between a high HEPA class and the next is smaller than it sounds, because both already capture the overwhelming majority of particles. What matters more is whether the machine seals around the filter, moves enough air and gets maintained. A well-sealed unit with a good filter outperforms a poorly sealed unit with a theoretically better one.

The part labels never mention: sealing

A filter can be excellent while the purifier is not. If the cabinet leaks, air bypasses the filter through gaps along the frame, and the effective performance falls. A good design uses a gasket and a snug housing so that the air must pass through the media.

You cannot read this from a label, but you can notice clues. A properly designed unit has a filter that seats firmly, a gasket along the edge and a cabinet without loose panels. Review the filter fit when you install a replacement, and make sure the frame is seated all the way in before you close the cover.

Airflow matters more than the label

A perfect filter on a feeble fan cleans a room slowly. Clean air delivery depends on how much air passes through the filter each minute and how efficiently the filter captures particles at that flow rate. That is why the AHAM figure described in our CADR explainer is the better comparison between machines than the HEPA label alone.

When you read a product page, treat HEPA as a gate. If the filter is claimed to meet the definition, you have passed the gate. Then compare machines on delivery rate, noise, filter cost and sealing, which decide the real result.

What HEPA filtration does not do

  • It does not remove gases, odors or volatile chemicals. Those need an adsorbent, covered in our carbon filter article.
  • It does not kill microbes by itself. It traps particles, and microbes caught on the filter are not necessarily destroyed.
  • It does not clean air that never reaches it. Distance, closed doors and low fan speed limit what the machine can do.
  • It does not make a filter last forever. Loaded filters restrict airflow and need replacement.

These limits are not criticisms of HEPA. They explain why a HEPA label should be the start of an evaluation and not the end.

Extras sold alongside HEPA

Many purifiers add ionizers, UV lamps, photocatalytic filters or plasma features. These are marketed as bonuses, but they deserve a second look. Some produce ozone as a byproduct, which is a lung irritant, and some provide little demonstrable benefit over the main filter.

If you have asthma or a sensitivity to airborne irritants, prefer a machine whose main job is mechanical filtration with a recognized filter, and treat any ionizing feature as optional and ideally switchable off. Check whether the manufacturer states that the unit meets limits on ozone emission, and be cautious where it does not.

Washable and permanent filters

Some products advertise a permanent or washable filter that never needs replacing. A genuine HEPA medium is generally not designed to be washed, because water can damage the fibers and reduce efficiency. Claims of washable HEPA should be verified against the manufacturer's test data.

A permanent filter that is not true filter media may be a coarser pre-filter. These are useful for catching lint and hair, but they are not a substitute for the HEPA stage. Look for a design that combines a washable pre-filter with a replaceable HEPA layer.

How to shop with this knowledge

  1. Ignore ladders. Do not assume True HEPA is better than HEPA.
  2. Check for a qualifier such as type, style or like. If you see one, the filter is not claimed to meet the definition.
  3. Look for a stated efficiency at a named particle size or a recognized class.
  4. Compare delivery rate, noise and filter cost.
  5. Check how the filter seals and how often it must be replaced.

How a fiber filter really captures particles

It is easy to picture a HEPA filter as a fine sieve, with holes smaller than the particles it blocks. That picture is wrong, and it leads to a common misunderstanding. The medium is a dense, random mat of very thin fibers, and the gaps between fibers are actually larger than many of the particles it captures.

Particles are caught because of how they move. Large and heavy particles cannot follow the airflow as it bends around a fiber, so they slam into it, which is called impaction. Medium particles follow the air but drift close enough to a fiber to touch it, which is interception. The smallest particles bounce around randomly, a motion called diffusion, and sooner or later they hit a fiber and stick.

The weak point sits between those regimes, where a particle is too small for impaction and too large for strong diffusion. The test size used in the HEPA definition targets that region, so a filter that does well there is likely to do well at both smaller and larger sizes. That is why the efficiency number is quoted at one size and still tells you a lot.

What happens as the filter loads

As a filter collects particles, the mat clogs gradually. Airflow through it falls, and the fan has to work harder or simply moves less air. Interestingly, a partly loaded filter can capture slightly more small particles, because the captured material adds more surfaces. Delivery, however, is falling at the same time.

This is why a replacement schedule matters even when the unit seems to be working. A fan that is running but moving little air gives the impression of operation without the result. If the airflow from the outlet feels weak, or the particle readings on the sensor do not improve, check the filter condition before you assume the machine is defective.

  • Check the filter indicator, if the unit has one, but treat it as an estimate.
  • Inspect the pre-filter monthly in a home with pets or dust.
  • Replace the HEPA layer on the manufacturer's schedule, sooner in smoky or dusty conditions.

Red flags on a product listing

A few patterns on a product page should make you slow down. None of them proves a product is bad, but together they point toward marketing doing the work that testing should do.

  • The only evidence of efficiency is the phrase True HEPA, with no stated test, class or particle size.
  • The filter is described with a hedge word such as type, style, like or grade.
  • The unit claims to remove an impossible share of everything, including gases, microbes and odors, with a single filter.
  • The listing relies on a medical, hospital or surgical phrase with no defined standard behind it.
  • The specification list has no delivery rate, noise figure or filter replacement interval.
  • Extra features such as ionizers are promoted as a main benefit and cannot be switched off.

A manufacturer that tells you exactly what the filter is, how it was tested and how often to replace it is giving you the information you need. Silence on those points is itself a signal.

Why the stack around the HEPA layer matters

A purifier is rarely just a HEPA filter. The typical stack starts with a coarse pre-filter, a washable mesh or a thin fabric that catches hair, lint and large dust. That layer protects the finer media behind it, so the HEPA stage clogs more slowly and lasts longer. A purifier without a pre-filter loads its main filter faster and may need replacement more often.

The stack may end with a carbon layer for odors. Each stage has a different job, and the product page should say what each one is and how big it is. A larger filter surface area gives the air more media to pass through, which lowers the resistance and helps the fan move more air quietly. Two units with the same HEPA claim can differ greatly in filter area, and the one with more area usually keeps its performance longer between changes.

When you compare machines in the best air purifiers guide, look past the single HEPA word and read the whole filter stack. It is a better predictor of real-world results and running cost than the label alone.

Medical grade and hospital claims

Phrases such as medical grade, hospital grade and surgical grade appear on many boxes. None of them corresponds to a defined consumer standard for air purifiers. Hospitals use engineered ventilation systems with filtration, pressure control and air exchange rates that a portable home unit does not replicate, and a home unit cannot be described as providing the same protection.

If a claim uses one of these phrases, look for the actual test behind it. A named test with a stated efficiency at a stated particle size is information. A medical phrase on its own is an adjective, and it should not move a purchase decision.

A thirty second label test

Open the product page and look for three things. First, does the filter description use a hedge word next to HEPA? Second, is there a stated efficiency, class or test named anywhere? Third, does the page give a delivery rate and a replacement interval? Two out of three is a reasonable page. One or none means you should look at a different listing, or at the manufacturer's own site, before you spend anything.

After you buy: getting the claimed performance

Even a well-filtered purifier only helps if it runs. Leave it on continuously at a speed you can tolerate, because particle levels rebound quickly once the machine stops. Close the doors of the room, keep the intake clear and replace filters on schedule. These habits matter more to real results than the difference between neighboring filter grades.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is True HEPA better than HEPA?

Not as a legal grade. True HEPA is a marketing phrase used to say that a filter meets the HEPA efficiency definition. A product labeled only as HEPA can be the same filter, so judge by the stated test or class.

What does HEPA-type mean?

It signals a filter that is not claimed to meet the HEPA definition. These filters can still trap particles, but with lower efficiency, so do not treat them as equivalent.

Does HEPA remove viruses and bacteria?

A HEPA filter traps particles of the size those organisms travel on, so it can remove them from air that passes through it. It does not guarantee that a room is free of microbes, and it does not disinfect the filter itself.

Is H13 the same as HEPA?

H13 is a European filter class that corresponds to high efficiency filtration, but it uses its own test method and numbers. It is not an exact synonym for the US wording, so compare only within the same system.

Can I wash a HEPA filter?

Most HEPA filters are not designed to be washed, and water can damage the fibers. Only wash a filter that the manufacturer states is washable, and expect to replace the HEPA layer on schedule.

How often should I replace a HEPA filter?

Follow the manufacturer's interval, which varies by model and use. Homes with smoke, pets or construction dust load filters faster and may need earlier changes.

Bottom line

True HEPA is a phrase, not a rung above HEPA. The real divide is between filters claimed to meet the HEPA efficiency definition and filters hedged with words such as type or like. Check the qualifiers, look for a stated test or class, then judge the machine on delivery rate, sealing, noise and filter cost. That route gets you a purifier that cleans air instead of just carrying a convincing label.
